Scored Spine Questions

Conversation 1: Activation and Attention
Getting started and staying with the thread.
1.1. When I know what to start, I still get stuck before the first action - even with time and quiet available.
1.2. Tasks I delayed starting this month became late, costly, or required visible apology or repair.
1.3. Getting started is hard on ordinary rested days too, not only during poor sleep, illness, high stress, or unusually heavy demand.
1.4. External structure - a deadline, another person present, or a written first step - reliably gets me started when planning alone does not.
1.5. During complex work, I lose the thread and need to reread, restart, or retrace my steps.
1.6. Attention drift this month produced visible errors, missed information, or work I had to redo.
1.7. My attention drifts on tasks I genuinely care about, not only on tasks that bore me.
1.8. My attention holds noticeably better when I use external scaffolds rather than relying on effort alone.

Conversation 2: Memory and Priority
Working memory, sequencing, and deciding what matters first.
2.1. In the middle of speaking or doing a task, I lose the exact word, number, instruction, or next step I was holding.
2.2. This past month, losing information from mind led to concrete mistakes, repeated work, missed details, or avoidable follow-up.
2.3. Memory gaps show up even on rested, ordinary-demand days, not only when sleep, illness, stress, or overload would explain them.
2.4. A capture system only helps if I use it immediately; if I wait, details often disappear before I can record them.
2.5. I see everything that needs doing but cannot decide what to do first, so I do nothing or the wrong thing.
2.6. Prioritization breakdowns this month caused missed deadlines, dropped balls, or work on the wrong thing first.
2.7. Even with a clear list and ranked priorities, the ordering itself still feels mentally costly.
2.8. I prioritize better when someone helps me triage out loud or when I limit choices to two.

Conversation 3: Time and Switching
Time estimation, transitions, interruptions, and context switching.
3.1. I consistently misjudge how long tasks take, even on work I have done many times before.
3.2. Time-estimation errors this month produced lateness, rushed quality, or missed commitments that other people noticed.
3.3. Time slips happen on familiar, well-practiced tasks too - not only on new or unusually complex ones.
3.4. External time anchors noticeably tighten my time estimates when I use them, more than self-discipline does.
3.5. Switching between tasks costs me disproportionate time and mental friction before I can re-engage.
3.6. This past month, interruptions made me lose work state enough that I missed steps, duplicated work, or left tasks unfinished.
3.7. Even planned switches between familiar tasks leave a restart cost before I can think clearly again.
3.8. Batching similar tasks or using a short transition ritual reduces restart cost more reliably than simply trying harder.

Conversation 4: Load and Cognitive Recovery
Emotion under demand and recovery after overload.
4.1. When mental demand spikes, my emotional reactions intensify - irritation, tearfulness, or shutdown - beyond what the moment calls for.
4.2. Tipping over emotionally this month cost me a conversation, a relationship, or work I cared about.
4.3. The reaction is tied to mental demand itself; it can happen even when the situation is not emotionally important.
4.4. Reducing input in the moment - pausing the conversation, lowering noise, or writing the next step - helps the reaction settle.
4.5. After a mentally demanding day that used to be manageable, I now need substantially more recovery before clear thinking returns.
4.6. This month, recovery after cognitive demand took long enough that the next day's work, home, or relationship responsibilities suffered.
4.7. The recovery lag is happening after normal demands for my life, not only after unusually intense weeks.
4.8. Planned recovery before and after demanding periods changes how long the depletion lasts.

Conversation 5: Sleep and Hormonal Variability
Restoration, body-state patterns, and midlife variability.
5.1. Even with adequate hours, I wake unrested or take a long time to feel mentally online.
5.2. Within a day or two of fragmented or unrestorative sleep, my focus, mood, or judgment changes enough that I notice it.
5.3. Sleep disruption often has a physical driver I can name, such as temperature, night sweats, body agitation, bathroom trips, alcohol, or late caffeine.
5.4. When sleep is more consolidated for two or more nights, next-day focus, mood, or word-finding noticeably improves.
5.5. My focus, mood, or energy has recognizable swings across the month or transition stage, even if I cannot predict exact days.
5.6. This past month, a body-state swing produced a day below my usual functioning baseline.
5.7. The swings line up more with cycle changes, vasomotor symptoms, sleep disruption, or hormone-related treatment changes than with workload alone.
5.8. Tracking sleep, symptoms, timing, and demand helps me plan better days and lower-demand days more accurately.

Conversation 6: Energy and Stress Physiology
Daily energy stability and stress-system rebound.
6.1. My energy is unpredictable across the day - strong stretches followed by abrupt drops without clear cause.
6.2. Energy crashes this month forced me to cancel, postpone, or under-deliver on plans that mattered.
6.3. Crashes happen on days with adequate sleep, food, and reasonable demand - not only on overloaded days.
6.4. Planning demanding work inside known higher-energy windows improves outcomes more than scheduling by calendar convenience.
6.5. My body stays revved up after stress passes - restless, tense, or wired-but-tired even when the trigger is over.
6.6. Staying revved up after stress this month interfered with my sleep, focus, digestion, or ability to enjoy downtime.
6.7. My body revs up even at small or imagined stressors, not only during genuinely demanding events.
6.8. A concrete downshift - walking, breathing, quiet, shower, or movement - reduces body activation within about 30 minutes.

Conversation 7: Fuel Rhythm and Body Load
Caffeine, appetite, glucose rhythm, pain, tension, and somatic burden.
7.1. When I go too long without food, or when caffeine timing is off, my thinking changes within a few hours.
7.2. This month, food or caffeine timing contributed to a crash, irritability, or scattered thinking that changed what I could get done.
7.3. Focus or mood shifts happen even when the task is familiar; food, hydration, or caffeine timing is the clearer variable.
7.4. A predictable meal rhythm, hydration plan, or caffeine cutoff changes the afternoon pattern enough for me to notice.
7.5. Body discomfort pulls attention away from thinking, such as pain, headaches, gut symptoms, temperature shifts, or tension.
7.6. This month, body symptoms changed what I could attend, finish, tolerate, or recover from.
7.7. Body symptoms worsen in recognizable conditions such as poor sleep, heat, stress load, cycle or transition changes, or prolonged sitting.
7.8. A specific body support I can name reduces the distraction enough to improve functioning.

Conversation 8: Medication, Substances, and Rebound
Medication or supplement effects and return-to-baseline recovery.
8.1. Something I take or use clearly changes my focus, mood, sleep, or energy.
8.2. This month, side effects, wearing-off, rebound, or timing effects from something I take or use disrupted my day.
8.3. The pattern seems linked to timing, missed doses, dose changes, or wearing-off periods more than to workload alone.
8.4. When timing is consistent, I can tell more clearly whether the pattern improves, worsens, or stays the same.
8.5. After ordinary effort or a busy week, my body takes longer to recover than it used to.
8.6. Slow physical recovery this month meant cancelled plans, lost weekends, or under-functioning at work.
8.7. Recovery has slowed compared with my prior baseline at the same workload - not only after exceptional weeks.
8.8. Planned recovery before and after demanding periods changes the length of the crash.
